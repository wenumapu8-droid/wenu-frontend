Most people arrive with a screenshot.

A photograph of someone else's ear, saved from an app, carrying six or seven placements and a promise: *this is what I want.* It is a good starting point. It is not a plan. The ear in that image belongs to a different body — a different fold of cartilage, a different depth of conch, a different way of sleeping and healing and living.

An ear is not a surface. It is a terrain.

## Anatomy first

Not every ear can hold every piercing. This is the part that gets skipped, and it is the part that decides everything else.

A rook needs a ridge with enough depth for the jewelry to sit *inside* it, not perch on top. A daith needs a particular curve to accept a ring without pressure. A flat needs actual surface area. A conch needs enough bowl that the piece does not lever against the edge every time a pillow touches it.

When anatomy does not support a placement, forcing it does not produce a piercing. It produces migration, prolonged irritation, embedded jewelry, or a piece that never quite sits right and never quite looks like the screenshot either.

So sometimes the honest answer is: *not this one.* Followed immediately by the useful part — *here is what your ear does support, and here is what it will do better than the ear in that photograph.*

An anatomical no is not a refusal. It is the first piece of real information you get.

## Then the sequence

Ears are not built in a session. They are built over years, and the healing is not an inconvenience between purchases — it is the actual work.

Cartilage takes months. Not the two weeks a lobe takes; months, sometimes the better part of a year before a piercing is genuinely settled rather than merely quiet. That timeline is not negotiable by wanting it faster.

Which means placing four cartilage piercings in one afternoon creates four simultaneous healing sites, competing for blood supply on the same small structure, on the same side of the head you sleep on. Each one makes the others harder. It is the single most common reason a curated ear goes wrong — not bad placement, not bad jewelry, just too much at once.

A curation that arrives across three sessions and eighteen months will look better than one bought in an afternoon. It will also still be there in five years, which the fast version frequently is not.

There is a second reason to go slowly. Downsizing. The jewelry worn during initial healing is deliberately longer, to allow for swelling. Once the swelling resolves, that length becomes a liability — it snags, it angles, it moves. Downsizing to a correctly fitted post is standard practice, and it is a separate appointment. Build the sequence knowing those appointments exist.

## Then, finally, composition

Only now does design enter.

A curated ear is not a maximum number of piercings. It is a relationship between them: the rhythm of scale, the intervals of empty skin, where the eye rests and where it travels. Three well-placed pieces with considered spacing read as intentional. Nine crowded pieces read as accumulation.

This works the way a constellation works. A constellation is not remarkable because every star in it is bright. It is remarkable because the spacing means something — because someone looked at scattered points and found a figure in the intervals.

The empty parts of the ear are doing as much work as the occupied ones.

## Material, briefly

Composition assumes the pieces will still be there. That depends on material more than on design.

For anything healing, implant-grade titanium (ASTM F-136) or solid gold — never plated, never mystery-metal, never surgical steel sold as if it were the same thing. Threadless or internally threaded, so no thread passes through the tissue. This is not a preference. It is the difference between a piercing that heals and one that argues with you for a year.

Once healed, the field opens considerably — and that is where the rest of the catalogue lives.

## Why we built it as a map

We made **Design Your Ear** so this conversation can begin before you are in the chair.

You place what you already have, what is currently healing, and what you are imagining. What comes back is a projection: a suggested sequence, an order of operations, an estimate of what it involves.

It is a proposal, not a promise. Every anatomy is different, and the final assessment happens in person, with your ear in front of us — because a tool cannot palpate cartilage or see how your conch actually sits. What the map removes is the guesswork: arriving with a screenshot and hoping.

The body is territory. Reading it well is the first act of respect.
