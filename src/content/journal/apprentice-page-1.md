I did not learn this in a school. I learned it on myself, at sixteen.

The first ones were lobes. Then a second lobe, then a helix. Then I started stretching my own ears, because once you have decided your body is somewhere you are allowed to work, you do not stop at one hole.

I was living in Italy. There was a small costume-jewelry shop in the centre that sold piercing jewelry, and a few steps away a fast-food restaurant with a bathroom. That bathroom is where my friends lined up for me. Septums, lobes, helixes. I worked with a cannula — an IV catheter, the kind anyone can buy without being asked why.

There were others like me, learning the same way, in the same kind of rooms. It was not rare. For a long time that was simply how the knowledge moved: hand to hand, without permission.

Every time I went back to Chile I pierced myself again. Another placement, another angle, another thing to understand from the inside. My own body was the practice ground.

## What I actually did wrong

Almost everything.

No sterile field — a public bathroom is close to the worst room a person can choose. No autoclave, and no way to prove anything was truly clean. No training in bloodborne pathogens; I did not know then what I know now about what can move between two people through one needle. No real understanding of anatomy: I placed by eye, by what looked right, not by reading the tissue underneath. No idea about jewelry material, so whatever the shop sold is what went in. No downsizing, because I had never heard the word.

Nobody got infected. I want to say that plainly, because it is true — and then say the more important thing, which is that it does not mean we were careful. We did not have the information to even define the risk properly. We were lucky more than we were good.

That sentence has stayed with me for twenty years.

## The detour

Then I stopped, for a while. I wanted to tattoo instead. I bought a machine.

And I found out how hard it actually is. Not hard the way I expected. What defeated me was the position of the machine in the hand — the angles, reaching the correct one and holding it — and knowing how much ink to carry. The machine was Chinese and cheap, the ink was bad, and I know now that some of what I blamed on myself was the equipment. But not all of it. Most of it was that I had walked up to a craft from outside and assumed the difficulty was visible from there.

It never is. Every hour of someone else's ease is years you have not done yet.

So I went back to piercing — not because tattooing beat me, but because standing next to another craft showed me what mastery actually costs, and made me want to pay it properly in the one my hands already knew.

## The rule I was obeying

Except I did not go back right away. That took much longer, and the reason was never skill.

I believed piercers were poor people.

That was the rule. Not stated anywhere, never argued with me directly, simply absorbed: that this was something you did, not something you became. That a life had a correct shape, and metal through skin was not the material you build one out of.

The strange part is that I already had the evidence against it. I had seen the heads carrying gold — real gold, a lot of it, in placements that cost more than most people's jewelry. I knew there was abundance in this world. I could see it. And I still believed the other thing, because a belief you absorb young does not wait for evidence.

At home it was read as a phase. Not cruelly — just the way a parent reads a teenager: the piercings alongside the hardcore, the emo, the screamo, all of it filed together as something that would pass. They were not being unkind. They were reading it with the information they had, and the information said this was a season, not a direction.

So I did what you are supposed to do. I studied. Industrial design, then orfebrería — a municipal goldsmith's workshop, basic to advanced, ending in Mapuche silverwork. A state craft grant and the tools that came with it. Years at the Ruka in La Cisterna. Real training, real hands, work that lives inside everything I make now.

None of that was wasted. But underneath it I was still waiting to feel prepared for the other thing, and that feeling does not arrive. What arrives, if you are lucky, is the day you notice the rule you have been obeying was never yours.

When that broke, I came back and studied this the way I had studied the silver. Properly. Bloodborne pathogens. Heartsaver. The courses, the conferences, the standards I had spent twenty years not knowing existed.

That is when I stopped being someone who pierces and started becoming a piercer. The difference is training, and it took years.

## What I would say to that kid

I have thought about this, and the answer surprised me.

I would not tell him to stop.

I would tell him he was right about the one thing everyone around him had wrong — that this was not a phase, that his hands knew something, that the people telling him otherwise did not have information he lacked; they had a rule he had not yet examined. I would tell him to fight for it, and not to hand the decision to people who could not see what he could see.

And then I would take the cannula out of his hand.

Because he was right about the calling and wrong about the room. Those are two different things, and it took me two decades to separate them.

## What I would say to you

Do not do what I did.

Not because it was rebellious and I have gone soft — because I know exactly what I was risking, and I did not know it at the time. A cannula in an unsterile room can give you hepatitis. An angle placed by eye can migrate for years, scar, or reject. The wrong metal keeps a piercing angry for months while you wonder what you did wrong. That nobody was hurt is not a defence; it is a debt I did not know I was taking on.

If you want a piercing, go to someone who can show you their sterilization records, their bloodborne pathogen certification, and the material specification of what is going into you. Ask them directly. Anyone good will be glad you asked.

And if what you want is to *learn* this — the road exists now, properly, and it is far better than the one I took. Write to me and I will tell you where it starts.

## Why this trade looks the way it does

Body piercing did not come out of clinics. It came out of punk, out of DIY, out of communities who marked themselves because nobody else would do it for them. The reason it professionalized — the reason there is an Association of Professional Piercers, bloodborne pathogen certification, implant-grade material standards, sterilization you can actually verify — is that people like me were doing this in bathrooms, and eventually somebody got hurt.

The standards are not a betrayal of that origin. They are what that origin learned.

So I keep both: the lineage that put a needle in my hand at sixteen, and the training that taught me I had no business holding it yet.

## One more thing, from the same year

I gave myself a septum at sixteen too. And then I hid it for two years.

Hiding a septum is ordinary for a teenager — you flip it up, nobody knows. What was maybe less ordinary is that I stretched it to eight millimetres while it was hidden. Eight millimetres is not a secret piercing. It is a quiet, daily, two-year act of engineering that no one around me could see.

I have thought about why I did that, and the answer is the whole reason this brand exists. I was not doing it to be seen. There was no audience — that was the point of hiding it. I was doing it because the body was the one territory that belonged entirely to me, and I wanted to know what it could hold.

The rule said this was a phase for other people to watch and wait out. But under my own nose, literally, I was already living the answer, in private, years before I let myself say it out loud.

The kid was never confused about what he wanted. He just took a long time to believe he was allowed to want it in daylight.
