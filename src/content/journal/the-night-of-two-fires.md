There are two fires in the sky on the same night this August, and they arrive from opposite directions.

The first is a subtraction. On the 12th, the Moon slides fully across the Sun and, for a little over two minutes, the daylight is taken back. The path of totality crosses Greenland, Iceland and Spain — the first total solar eclipse to touch mainland Europe since 1999. A covered sun is not darkness exactly; it is a different light, the kind that makes birds go quiet and the horizon glow at the wrong hour.

The second fire is an addition. The same night, the Perseids reach their peak — the year's most generous meteor shower, falling near a new moon, which means the dark is deep enough to see them clearly. From the northern sky, and from the bench here in Truckee, you can lie back and count the streaks: old dust from a comet, burning up as it enters the air.

One sky, closing and opening at once.

## The sun, covered

Every culture that watched the sky long enough built a story for the moment the sun disappears. Not because they lacked the mechanics — many tracked the cycles with precision — but because the mechanics were never the whole of it. To lose the sun, even for two minutes, is to be reminded that the source can be withdrawn. That the light you build your day around is on loan.

We don't borrow anyone else's myth to explain it. But we keep the instinct behind them: that the sky is not scenery. It is an agent. It gives and it takes and it keeps time, and the oldest human response to that has always been to mark the body in answer.

## Metal that fell

The meteors of the Perseids burn up before they land. But not everything the sky drops is lost. Some of it reaches the ground and stays — iron that fell as fire and cooled into stone.

That is the material at the center of our work. The meteorite in an Atacama piece is not a metaphor for the cosmos; it *is* the cosmos, arrived. When we forge it into something you can wear, we are only continuing a very old exchange — the sky sends something down, and a person carries it on the body, close to the skin, where it can be felt.

A night like this one makes the exchange visible. Fire falls. Some of it we forge.

## The oldest calendar

Long before there were printed almanacs, the sky was the calendar. The turning of the seasons, the phases of the moon, the return of a shower on the same nights each year — these were how a person knew where they stood in the year, and in their life.

The body kept pace with it. Ornaments marked the solstice, the passage, the belonging. The oldest pierced adornments we have found are eleven thousand years old; the oldest preserved body wearing stretched lobes is more than five. The practice is not new. What is new is only that most of us stopped reading the sky, and started reading screens instead.

This month, for one night, the sky insists on being read. The sun will go dark over the north. The Perseids will fall. You don't need a piece from us to watch it — only a clear horizon and the patience to look up.

But if you want to carry a little of that night with you afterward, we know where the fallen fire is kept. We work it by hand.

*— From the studio, Truckee, California.*
